
## Take 1 Hour to do this to feel better the rest of the year

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:2000/1*MFoSBuptkqEjvtm-6UubQA.jpeg)

**Source**: Photo by Author

Every January, I set aside about an hour to go through all my devices and clean things up. I don’t do this because some productivity blog told me to or because something is broken.

It just feels like the right time. New year, clean slate. It’s kind of like how people do a spring cleaning at home, even if nothing is really wrong.

I’ve noticed that this one hour makes a difference for months. My devices feel faster, I stop getting annoyed by small things I was ignoring, and I know where everything is again. That last part is actually more important than it sounds.

Here’s what I actually do every year.

### Understanding What Each Device Is For

This might sound obvious, but I think most people end up using their devices for everything instead of giving each one a clear job.

I have more Apple devices than I probably need, so I have to be careful about this or I’d just get overwhelmed.

My iPhone 17 Pro is my main device.

It goes everywhere with me, all my daily apps are on it, and the camera is honestly amazing.

But I also have an iPhone 13 Pro Max that mostly stays at home, and giving it a specific job changed how I use both phones.

The 13 Pro Max is where I test iOS betas and apps I want to try out, so my main phone stays stable and doesn’t get filled up with random apps I installed just once.

I also keep it on my bedside table for reading at night, and the important thing is that it has no social media apps at all. When I pick it up in bed, I can read in Apple Books, catch up on Substack, check newsletters in Spark Mail, or read articles on Medium.

What I can’t do is open Instagram and lose 45 minutes to reels. That one choice probably saves me an hour of sleep every week.

I do the same thing with my Macs. The M4 Mac Mini is my main work computer where I do everything important. The M1 MacBook Pro is just for testing apps and for travel, so my Mini stays clean.

I used to install everything on one computer, but splitting up my work machine and testing machine means my main computer never has some random app I downloaded months ago still running in the background.

It took me longer to figure out what to do with my watches. I have an Apple Watch Ultra 2 that I only wear when I go out, and an Apple Watch Series 5 that just stays on a wireless charger on my analog desk.

That Series 5 isn’t really a watch anymore. It’s more like a smart clock that shows me calls and messages. I keep it on my analog desk, so I don’t have to bring my phone there.

The point isn’t that you need a bunch of devices. The point is that every device should have a clear job, and January is when I check if those jobs still make sense.

### The iPhone Cleanup

I have about 80 apps installed and probably use only 20 of them regularly.  
Every January, I go through and delete any app I haven’t opened in the past 3 months. It sounds harsh, but it’s not, and if I need something again, I can just reinstall it in thirty seconds.

What I can’t get back is the time and energy I waste scrolling past apps I never use when I’m just looking for the ones I actually need.  
My home screen has two pages.

The first page has the apps I use multiple times a day, plus widgets that give me quick info like Gumroad sales, pages read in my book tracker, hydration from WaterLlama, focus sessions from Forest, habits from Streaks, and a Siri Suggestions widget that’s actually pretty accurate. It shows Craft in the morning and Books at night without me even telling it to.

The second page has apps I use daily but not constantly, plus a couple more widgets.

No folders. I tried using folders for years and finally realized I was just hiding apps I didn’t want to deal with. Now, everything either gets a spot on those two pages or it goes to the App Library, where I’ll find it if I ever need it.

One thing I can’t stand is seeing red badge notifications on my home screen, so I clear them all the time. Sometimes email builds up for a day or two, but that’s about it. I’m not saying you should do this, it’s just something I do that keeps my phone looking clean.

### The Downloads Folder Situation

My Mac’s Downloads folder has about 300 files right now. There are images I saved for reference, software installers I only used once, PDFs from projects that ended months ago, and screenshots I should have deleted the day I took them.

This happens every year, no matter how careful I try to be.

Fixing it takes about fifteen minutes. I sort by date added, quickly check anything older than three months, move the few things I actually need to the right folders in iCloud Drive, and delete everything else. Then I empty the trash so I don’t second-guess myself.

I’ve tried things like automatic rules, weekly cleanups, and different folder setups to stop this from happening. But honestly, none of them stuck.  
What works for me is just accepting that this folder will get messy and cleaning it up once a year. Sometimes the best system is just knowing you need to do it at all.

### Photos and Screenshots

I have about 22,000 photos and almost 3,000 screenshots.

I mostly leave my photos alone because they’re real memories, and I use iCloud Photos to sync everything. But screenshots are a different story.

They pile up like browser tabs, each one feeling important at the time and then meaningless a few weeks later.

Most of my screenshots are app interfaces I saved for blog posts, random Instagram saves, tweets I thought I’d use later, and things I honestly don’t even remember taking.

This usually clears out a few hundred screenshots and makes my photo library feel less like a junk drawer.

### Subscriptions

I pay for about ten subscriptions through the App Store, plus Apple One on the family plan. Every January, I go to Settings, tap my name, tap Subscriptions, and check for anything I’m paying for but haven’t opened in a couple of months.

The question I ask is simple: if this subscription ended today, would I even notice this week? Not if I might want it back someday, just if I’d notice right now. If the answer is no, I cancel it. I can always resubscribe if I was wrong.

### Notifications

I have maybe ten apps that are allowed to send me notifications. That’s it.  
Every January, I scroll through Settings, then Notifications, and look for anything I don’t remember turning on or anything that’s been annoying me. Most apps don’t need to interrupt me. Email, messages, calls, calendar reminders, yes. That game I downloaded last month, definitely not.

The one notification I always forget to turn off is the drinking water reminder. I use WaterLlama to track hydration, and it’s great, but I don’t need my watch buzzing every hour telling me to drink water. I know I should drink water. The reminder just nags me.

I use Focus modes, but I stopped trying to make complicated setups with different modes for everything. I just have one Focus mode called Work that turns on automatically at certain times. That’s it. I’ve tried the complex setups with custom home screens and app restrictions, but they never stick. One automatic mode is enough for me.

### NFC Tags

==I have NFC tags around my house that run Shortcuts when I tap my phone on them. One on my desk starts a focus timer. One on my water bottle logs my water intake. One on my mirror plays music. One near my bed turns off the TV and lights.==

In January, I just check that each one still works, since iOS updates sometimes break Shortcuts in weird ways, and think about whether I need any new ones. Usually I don’t. The simpler this system is, the more I actually use it.

### The Visual Reset

I change my iPhone wallpaper pretty often during the year, mostly using Backdrops or wallpapers I made myself. But in January, I think about it more on purpose.

What I’ve learned is that wallpapers matter more than I thought they would.

A busy wallpaper with lots of colors and details makes my home screen feel cluttered, even if I only have ten apps. A simple wallpaper with muted colors makes the same home screen feel calm.

I don’t have any science to back this up, just years of noticing how different wallpapers change how I feel when I pick up my phone.

My Mac wallpapers are usually just Apple’s defaults because I honestly don’t see my desktop that much. I don’t try to match wallpapers across devices.

### Looking Back at Last Year’s Notes

This is the part most people skip, but I think it’s actually the most valuable part of the whole process.

I open Apple Notes and Craft and scroll through everything from the past year. I just scan the titles and try to remember what I was thinking about in March, what projects I started in July, and what ideas I had in October.

I also look for finished projects (what actually got done), ideas I abandoned (why did I stop, and is it worth bringing back), and things that changed (how is my life different now compared to last January).

Most of the notes get archived or deleted. Old meeting notes, random thoughts that went nowhere, half-finished outlines for things I never wrote. The few things worth keeping, I move to a 2026 folder.

This reflection part takes maybe twenty minutes, but it gives me more clarity about the year ahead than any planning exercise I’ve tried. You can’t figure out where you’re going if you don’t know where you’ve been.

I have a desk at home where I don’t allow any screens. Just notebooks, pens, books, and that Apple Watch Series 5 on its charger showing me the time and notifications. I spend thirty minutes to an hour there most days, journaling, planning, brainstorming, reading real books, and taking handwritten notes.

### The Apps I’ll Delete and Reinstall

Every January, I delete Instagram and YouTube from my main iPhone. By February, at least one of them is back.

But the break helps. It reminds me that these apps are choices, not defaults. And when I reinstall them, I’m a bit more aware of when and why I’m opening them.

### What This Actually Gives You

Spending an hour on this once a year makes my devices feel new without spending any money. Everything feels lighter, more organized, and more like mine. The steps matter less than just going through everything on purpose instead of letting it pile up.

If you do nothing else, do this: pick up your phone right now and delete one app you haven’t opened in three months. Just one. Notice how it feels. That feeling, across your whole setup, is what I’m looking for every January.
